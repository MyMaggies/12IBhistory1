GATE3
MINUTES 12–20 · QUESTION FORGE

Forge a question worth chasing

"What was it like?" is a great start and a terrible research question, because you would never finish. Historians upgrade their questions until the evidence can actually answer them, and until a reasonable person could disagree with the answer.

The question ladder

Here is one for Paris in 1789. Aim for rung 3 or 4.

RUNG 1
When did the Bastille fall?Fact. Answered in five seconds. Nothing to research.
RUNG 2
What was Paris like in 1789?Describe. A list of facts. Where would you even stop?
RUNG 3
Why did ordinary Parisians turn on the king in 1789?Explain. Needs evidence and an argument.
RUNG 4
Who gained the most from the fall of the Bastille, and who lost?Debate. Historians will disagree, and that is the fun part.

The forge

Pick a thinking tool. Then tap a starter to load it, and finish the blanks (______) so it fits your place and time.

Field Notes · Box 3

Write your first question ("what was it like?" is allowed), then your upgraded question. Tick the checks it passes.

Teacher · Gate 3 · 8 min

  • Circulate with one question: "Could two historians answer that differently?" If not, send them up a rung.
  • Common problems: too big ("Why did Rome fall?") means narrow the place and time; too factual means add "why" or "who gained".
  • The four tools are the IB concepts in plain words: cause and consequence, continuity and change, perspectives, significance. You do not need to name them yet.
  • Have two or three students read their upgraded question aloud. Accept imperfect, since it will be revised at Gate 6.
GATE4
MINUTES 20–32 · SOURCE DETECTIVE

Find what was really there

Time for evidence about . Learn the one big trick with sources first (about three minutes), then go hunting on your iPad.

Primary or secondary? It depends on your question.

The quick rule (a good first guess)

Primary = made at the time, by someone who was there. Secondary = made later, looking back.

The historian's rule

A source is not primary or secondary all by itself. It is primary or secondary for a question. Ask: for what I am studying, is this from the time and the people involved, or is it a later or second-hand account?

A soldier’s diary, written in the trenches in 1916

Studying life in the trenchesPRIMARY
Studying the Tsar’s rule in 1905SECONDARY

A newspaper article about a crime

Studying the crimeSECONDARY
Studying the newspaper and its editorsPRIMARY

Your turn: same source, two questions

Your mission: find two sources, then label them for your question

Source 1 · from the time

A diary, letter, newspaper, photograph, poster, map, law, object, speech, made when it happened. If you cannot find one, that is a finding: whose voices survived?

Source 2 · looking back

An article, museum page, documentary, or a historian's summary made later about your place and time.

Then label themIs each source primary or secondary for your question?

Bonus: what question could you ask that would flip Source 2 from secondary to primary?

Question every source with three detective questions

WHO?Who made it, and were they there? What did they stand to gain?
WHEN and WHY?When was it made and who was it for? To record, to persuade, to sell, to remember?
WHAT'S MISSING?What can this not tell me? Whose voice is absent?

      Teacher · Gate 4 · 12 min

      • Primary vs secondary (3 min): the page teaches the quick rule first, then the sharper one. A source is primary or secondary for a question: a trench diary is primary for trench life but secondary for the Tsar's 1905 reign; a crime report is secondary for the crime but primary for the newspaper and its editors. Expect a good argument about edge cases (a participant's memoir written decades later). Reward the reasoning, not the label.
      • Add a line that primary does not mean reliable and secondary does not mean useless. Both still get the three detective questions.
      • The "probably out there / harder to find" panel changes with the departure. For a wildcard trip it adjusts to the year typed on the pass. Treat it as informed guesswork, not a promise.
      • Hunt (8 min): watch for Wikipedia-only research and point to the references. Check students are noting dates on sources.
      • If a student cannot find a primary source, praise it: "Why might there be none?" leads into who could write, who was recorded, and who kept records.
      • This is the raw material for Paper 1 (content, context) and for the IA (choosing and analysing sources).

      MINUTES 32–40 · LENS SWITCH

      Whose history are you getting?

      Every history you read has a lens: a decision about what is worth noticing. Change the lens and the same place and time looks different. Tap a lens to see what it reveals, and what it hides.

      Your two-minute experiment

      1. Be honest: which lens did your first search results use?
      2. Pick two different lenses. Search again for with each in mind.
      3. Find one detail per lens. Which lens told you the most? What did each one leave out?
      Field Notes · Box 5

      Write your two lenses, one detail from each, and one thing each lens hides.

      Teacher · Gate 5 · 8 min

      • Ask "which lens dominated your search results?" Many will say Big Names. Follow with: who wrote the records, and who could?
      • This is where content meets concepts: what was significant, for whom, and what changed for whom.
      • Two or three students share the detail they found with the second lens.
      GATE6
      MINUTES 40–52 · THE CLASH

      Find the argument

      History is not just facts. It is arguments about what the facts mean. Two accounts of the same thing can disagree, and working out why is half the skill.

      Different evidenceThey used different sources, or different parts of the record.
      Different lensThey cared about different things: leaders, workers, money, ideas.
      Different timeThey wrote in different eras, with different values and questions.
      Different purposeOne wanted to persuade, another to record, another to sell.

      Spot the reason (2 minutes)

      Your mission: find a real clash

      Find two accounts of that disagree, or emphasise very different things. Then decide why they differ and which you trust more.

      Add debate, controversy, myth, "historians disagree" to your topic.
      vsCompare a museum page with a documentary, or a national history with another country's view.
      then/nowCompare something from the time with a modern historian.

      Now give your verdict

      I think _______ because _______, but _______ makes it more complicated.

      A good answer has a because and a but. That is what an argument looks like.

      Field Notes · Box 6

      Record Claim A and Claim B, tick why they differ, say which you trust more, then write your because…but verdict.

      Teacher · Gate 6 · 12 min

      • Matcher (3 min): these have a "best" answer but accept discussion. Time and purpose are the ones students under-use.
      • Hunt (7 min): if a student cannot find a clash, hand them one of the famous clashes. Two or three groups share quickly at the end.
      • Verdict (2 min): push for the "but". It is the difference between an opinion and an argument, and it mirrors what Paper 3 rewards.
